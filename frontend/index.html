How It Works

Combining deep learning computer vision with validated life-cycle emission intensity data.

01
Upload a photo
Take or select an image of your discarded waste item on any plain or contrasting surface.
02
Model identifies material
Our vision classifier (EfficientNetB0) extracts structural visual patterns and predicts the primary material class.
03
Factor lookup
The identified class is cross-referenced against empirical life-cycle assessment factors (min, average, max).
04
Multiplied by mass
The looked-up emission factor is multiplied by your verified weight to calculate the net carbon equivalent (kg CO₂e).

Why must you enter the item's weight?

A two-dimensional photograph captures surface texture, geometry, and color, but cannot determine an object's density, wall thickness, or mass. Entering an exact weight turns visual material classification into an accurate physical emission calculation.

Worked Example Calculation

Consider an aluminium beverage can weighing 250 g identified as Metal:

Weight: 250 g = 0.250 kg
Average Emission Factor (Metal): 2.365 kg CO₂e / kg
Estimated Footprint: 0.250 kg × 2.365 = 0.591 kg CO₂e
Empirical Range: [0.250 × 0.93 = 0.233 kg] to [0.250 × 3.80 = 0.950 kg CO₂e]

About the Project

Academic research initiative applying deep learning to accelerate consumer waste classification and transparent carbon accounting.

Motivation & Mission

Accurate waste segregation and carbon awareness remain major bottlenecks in circular resource stewardship. This project demonstrates how edge-compatible convolutional neural networks can automate material identification and directly link visual inspection to life-cycle greenhouse gas metrics.

Limitations & Frequently Asked Questions

Understanding the scope, constraints, and statistical assumptions behind our predictions.

Why do I need to enter the weight?
A photograph only reveals surface optical qualities (color, geometry, specular reflection), not volume or density. Without entering the exact mass, one cannot determine whether an aluminium object weighs 15 grams or 500 grams. Entering mass converts a unit emission factor (kg CO₂e / kg) into an absolute footprint.
What if my photo contains mixed waste or composite packaging?
The model identifies the predominant material visible in the frame. For composite items (such as juice cartons combining paper, plastic, and aluminium foil), classify the main material and use the manual subclass or class override dropdowns to reflect the dominant constituent.
Why is there a minimum and maximum range?
Carbon intensity varies significantly depending on alloy composition, virgin versus recycled feedstocks, manufacturing processes, and regional electrical grid mixes. The range bar visually illustrates this variability so users see the empirical uncertainty rather than a false sense of single-point precision.
Why might plastic grades be confused?
Many thermoplastic polymers (such as PET, HDPE, and PP) share transparent or translucent visual appearances that are difficult to distinguish purely from photographs. When two plastic grades have close prediction scores, the interface presents both alongside their respective footprints and prompts you to select the correct polymer.
How accurate is the classification model?
The fine-tuned EfficientNetB0 model achieved 94.4% accuracy and a 0.944 macro F1 score on our held-out test images. Real-world performance may vary depending on camera lighting, background clutter, and image clarity.
Are these calculation results exact carbon audit figures?
No. These calculations provide educated estimates intended for public awareness, education, and comparative benchmarking. They do not constitute a formal ISO 14040/14044 certified greenhouse gas audit.
